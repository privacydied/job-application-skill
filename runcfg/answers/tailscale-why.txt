I run a self-hosted Linux homelab as a matter of course — Docker services, monitoring,
backups, hardening — and most of my side projects are self-hosted end to end rather than
handed to a platform. So I've spent a lot of evenings on exactly the problem Tailscale
solves: getting machines that live in different places to talk to each other safely,
without standing up a fragile perimeter and hoping.

What draws me to Tailscale specifically is that it's an infrastructure product that takes
the human side seriously. That's the seam I work at. My background is design — six years
of it, including two and a half years as an interaction designer on the NHS COVID-19 App
inside the GOV.UK Design System, where plain language and accessibility were hard
requirements, not polish. But I also ship: Node.js and Postgres on gearo, a TypeScript
monorepo with a self-hosted vector basemap on fifteen.example.com, CI/CD and hosting at
CryptoKnowledge. Networking tools are usually built by people who already understand
networking, for people who already understand networking. Tailscale is one of the few that
reads as though someone cared whether it made sense to a person on their first day.

I also care about the privacy-by-design instinct behind it. On fifteen.example.com I built the
API to accept an H3 spatial cell rather than a coordinate, so it is structurally incapable
of receiving a user's exact location — the guarantee lives in the shape of the system, not
in a policy document. That's the same reasoning I see in a product that makes the secure
path the easy one.
