Two reasons.

The first is the problem space. Most product design is applied to low-stakes decisions;
Palantir's is applied where the decision matters and the user is accountable for it. I have
done that once already, on the NHS COVID-19 App at UKHSA, and it is the work I found most
worth doing — designing under real constraints, at national scale, where being clear is a
safety requirement rather than a preference.

The second is the way the work is organised. My strongest trait is that I sit at the
design–engineering seam: I do research and interaction design, and I also build — Node.js and
Postgres on my own price-comparison product, a TypeScript monorepo with spatial indexing and
a self-hosted vector basemap on another, CI/CD and hosting at CryptoKnowledge. Palantir's
forward-deployed model, where the person designing sits close to the real deployment and the
real user rather than at the end of a specification chain, matches how I already work.

On the products themselves, the part I would most want to work on is where a data model meets
an operator who has to justify a decision — the ontology-facing surfaces rather than the
dashboards. That is where design either makes a complex system legible or quietly hides the
uncertainty, and the second failure is the dangerous one.
